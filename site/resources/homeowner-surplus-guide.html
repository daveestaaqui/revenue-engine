Homeowner's Guide to Tax Auction Surplus Funds & Protecting Your Equity

Published by Surplus Docket Legal Intelligence • Updated September 2026 • Strict Non-Commercial Consumer Guidance

1. What Are Property Tax Auction Surplus Funds?

When real property is sold at a public county tax deed or tax lien foreclosure auction, the bidding frequently exceeds the outstanding delinquent property taxes, penalties, and administrative court costs. The excess monetary balance generated by this sale is legally termed tax surplus funds or excess proceeds.

Under United States constitutional law, these surplus funds belong to the former property owner or their rightful heirs, subject to legitimate junior lienholders (such as second mortgagees, judgment creditors, or municipal assessment liens) who held a recorded interest in the property at the time of sale.

2. Constitutional Rights: Tyler v. Hennepin County (2023)

On May 25, 2023, the Supreme Court of the United States issued a landmark unanimous decision in Tyler v. Hennepin County, 598 U.S. 631 (2023). The Court ruled that when a governmental entity sells a delinquent taxpayer's home to satisfy a tax liability, keeping the excess sales proceeds beyond the tax debt constitutes an unconstitutional taking without just compensation in violation of the Fifth Amendment.

Chief Justice Roberts, writing for the Court, famously affirmed: "The taxpayer must render unto Caesar what is Caesar's, but no more." As a consequence of this ruling, county governments across the nation are constitutionally required to make surplus funds available to the former titleholder.

⚠️ CRITICAL WARNING: Beware Unlicensed 'Asset Recovery' Finders

Following a tax deed sale, former owners are frequently targeted by unlicensed third-party "finders", "recovery agents", or "surplus auditors" who offer to collect the funds in exchange for a 30% to 50% contingency fee.

Before signing any contract or power of attorney:

  • You can claim surplus funds directly: County Clerk of Courts do not charge a fee to file an owner claim (beyond nominal statutory clerk registry or recording fees).
  • Third-party finder agreements may be illegal: Multiple states cap non-attorney fees or declare finder assignment contracts void under Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL) statutes.
  • Never sign a deed or blanket Power of Attorney: Predatory operators may attempt to transfer your underlying title rights rather than simply filing an administrative claim.

3. Statutory Filing Deadlines Across Key Jurisdictions

Surplus funds do not remain in the county court registry indefinitely. If not claimed within statutory limitation periods, the funds escheat to the state treasury or county general fund:

4. Legitimate Steps to Recover Your Excess Proceeds

  1. Contact Your County Clerk of Courts: Search for the Tax Deed or Excess Proceeds department in the county where your property was foreclosed. Request the official "Statement of Surplus" or "Notice of Excess Proceeds".
  2. Verify Outstanding Liens: Determine if there were unreleased mortgages, HOA liens, or municipal utility liens on the property prior to the auction. Subordinate lienholders have statutory priority over the owner in most jurisdictions.
  3. Obtain Proper Documentation: Prepare government-issued photo identification, proof of prior title (deed, probate decree, or settlement statement), and death certificate/letters of administration if claiming on behalf of a deceased relative.
  4. Consult Licensed Legal Counsel: If liens are disputed, multiple heirs exist, or substantial funds are held in the court registry, retain an attorney licensed in your state who specializes in foreclosure defense, real estate litigation, or probate law. Licensed attorneys are bound by strict state bar ethics and court fee regulations.
